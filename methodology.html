Methodology

A summary of the construction of the World Fiscal Uncertainty indices; the full treatment is Appendix B of the paper.

The indices follow the newspaper-based approach of Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016), with two changes. The policy term list is replaced by a fiscal one, so that the index counts articles about taxes, budgets, government spending, deficits and public debt rather than about policy in general. And the fixed set of national newspapers is replaced by the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph, so that one dictionary and one procedure produce comparable indices for about 200 economies — from each economy’s own press and from the press of other countries writing about it. The full treatment is Appendix B of the paper.

1. The corpus

  • Every web news article processed by GDELT from 1 January 2016 to 30 September 2026: 1,649,845,652 articles, of which 39 percent are in English and 61 percent are machine-translated from 63 other languages.
  • GDELT tags each article with themes from a fixed vocabulary that includes the Baker–Bloom–Davis term lists, applied to the translated text with one dictionary. Articles are therefore classified by their theme tags; no keyword list per language is needed.
  • The sample starts in 2016 because GDELT began applying the Baker–Bloom–Davis tags on 14 December 2015. The base period for scaling is 2016–2025.

2. Classifying articles

An article carries a lexicon \mathcal{L} if at least one of its themes belongs to it, L_a = \mathbb{1}\{\Theta(a)\cap\mathcal{L}\neq\varnothing\}. A fiscal policy uncertainty article carries the uncertainty, economy and fiscal lexicons at once:

\mathrm{FU}_a = U_a\,E_a\,F_a .

Uncertainty U EPU_UNCERTAINTY (U^+ adds USPEC_UNCERTAINTY1)
Economy E EPU_ECONOMY (E^+ adds EPU_ECONOMY_HISTORIC)
Fiscal, narrow F^- (4 themes) Baker–Bloom–Davis categories: taxes, fiscal policy, entitlement programs, debt ceiling and government shutdown
Fiscal (headline) F (20 themes) F^- plus sovereign-debt crises, GDELT’s tax, budget, spending, deficit, fiscal-policy, finance-ministry and public-investment themes, and the taxation, budget-deficit, sovereign-debt and austerity topics
Fiscal, broad F^+ (40 themes) F plus 20 World Bank public-finance topics (fiscal consolidation, sustainability, expenditure, tax policy, debt management, subsidies, …)
Policy, monetary, trade, sovereign debt P, M, T, S used for the companion series EPU =UEP, MPU =UEM, TPU =UET, SDU =US

The lexicons are nested, F^-\subset F\subset F^+. Themes that mention debt without a public-sector qualifier are excluded, because household and corporate debt dominate them. The exact tags are listed in Table B.1 of the paper.

3. Attribution of articles to economies

Each article is attributed to the country of its outlet (from GDELT’s list of 189,545 outlet domains, then the country-code domain) and to the countries it mentions (from its geocoded locations). For economy c:

View Code Articles counted Use
Domestic D published by outlets located in c domestic uncertainty; the counterpart of a national newspaper index (DFU)
Foreign X published outside c and mentioning c the external narrative read by foreign lenders and rating agencies (XFU)
All sources A every article mentioning c widest coverage; the smallest economies (AFU)
World W every article the global component (WFU)

Robustness versions restrict the domestic view to native-language articles (_NAT), English-language articles (_ENG) or articles that mention the country itself (_OWN), restrict any view to a stable outlet panel of outlets with at least 50 articles in every year 2016–2025 (_PNL), and restrict view A to articles mentioning at most two countries (_FOC).

4. From counts to shares

For each view and period, N is the number of articles and K the number of fiscal uncertainty articles among them. The share is a ratio of sums over the period (days, weeks, months, quarters or years), so that periods are weighted by their volume of news:

\hat\pi_{ct} = \frac{K_{ct}}{N_{ct}} .

Fiscal policy uncertainty articles are rare: about 2.2 per 1,000 articles, against 4.4 per 1,000 for economic policy uncertainty. For a small economy the raw share is a few articles divided by a few thousand, and its sampling variation is then of the same order as the variation the index is intended to measure.

5. Shrinkage (country series)

Following Efron and Morris (1975), the share is treated as a draw from a beta-binomial model centred on the economy’s own 2016–2025 pooled share \bar\pi_c, and replaced by its posterior mean

\tilde\pi_{ct} = \frac{K_{ct}+\kappa\,\bar\pi_{c}}{N_{ct}+\kappa},

where the prior precision \kappa (in articles) is estimated once per series by the method of moments. For the headline annual domestic series \hat\kappa = 488 articles: shrinkage is negligible for large economies and damps only the year-to-year noise of thin ones. Shrinking toward the economy’s own mean, not the cross-country mean, keeps the cross-country differences in level and does not manufacture comovement with the world series. World series are not shrunk.

6. Scaling

\mathrm{FU}_{ct} = 100\,\frac{\tilde\pi_{ct}}{\overline{\tilde\pi}_{c}}, \qquad \mathrm{FU\_Z}_{ct} = \frac{\ln\tilde\pi_{ct}-\mu_{c}}{\sigma_{c}},

where \overline{\tilde\pi}_{c}, \mu_c and \sigma_c are the 2016–2025 mean of the share and the mean and standard deviation of its logarithm, within each economy and at each frequency. The index (e.g. DFU) equals 100 on average over 2016–2025 in every economy and is the form for figures; the z-score (e.g. DFU_Z) is the form for regressions. The narrative gap is FUGAP_Z = XFU_Z − DFU_Z.

Flags. A cell is thin when the view has fewer than 1,000 articles or 10 fiscal uncertainty articles in a month (1,500 and 15 in a quarter; 2,000 and 20 in a year). COMPBRK flags cells where the all-outlet and stable-panel indices differ by more than a factor of two, a sign of a change in outlet composition.

Timing. Annual files also give the domestic, foreign and all-sources indices on the IMF WEO fiscal-year basis (_FY) and for the six pre-budget months before the fiscal year starts (_PB), labelled with the WEO fiscal year.

The largest months of the domestic series match the fiscal calendar of each economy — for example the pandemic months of 2020 in the United States, Germany and Brazil, the 2016 referendum in the United Kingdom, the 2018–2019 IMF programme months in Argentina, the postponed 2025 budget in South Africa, and April 2025, the month of the United States tariff announcements, in most economies.
